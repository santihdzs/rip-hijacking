\section{Reverse-Engineering the RIP MD5 Authentication}\label{sec:reveng}
\paragraph{Packet layout.} \ph{Main idea: each captured update is an
authenticated RIPv2 Response, decomposing into RIP header, auth header, route
entry, and auth trailer. Then, in order: each update is a RIPv2 Response
(command~2) sent to multicast 224.0.0.9 on UDP/520; authentication type~3 (Keyed
Message Digest) places an authentication header as the first route-table entry
and appends an authentication trailer carrying the digest; the observed size-06
packet decomposes as in \autoref{tab:layout}. Close on: the trailer's 16-byte
digest is the field the attack must reproduce.}

\paragraph{Digest construction (the key finding).} \ph{Main idea: the captured
digest is RFC~2082 keyed-MD5, not the HMAC-MD5 the brief names. Then, in order:
the 16-byte digest field is first overwritten with the key and a plain MD5 is
taken over the whole packet; define $D$ as the payload bytes from the RIP header
through the trailer's \texttt{AFI}/\texttt{type} field, i.e.\ everything except
the 16 digest bytes, and $K_{16}$ as the ASCII key right-padded with zero bytes
to 16, as used in \eqref{eq:keyed}. Close on: this construction and notation must
be stated before the equation.}
\begin{equation}
\text{digest} \;=\; \mathrm{MD5}\bigl(\, D \,\|\, K_{16} \,\bigr).
\label{eq:keyed}
\end{equation}
\ph{Main idea: how the construction and alphabet were confirmed by brute force.
Then, in order: we tested both HMAC and keyed-MD5 and both candidate alphabets;
only \eqref{eq:keyed} over a base-36 alphabet (lowercase letters and digits, not
hexadecimal) reproduced the captured digest; the hexadecimal space was exhausted
with no match. Close on: base-36 keyed-MD5 is the confirmed construction.}

\paragraph{Recovered key and validation.} \ph{Main idea: the recovered key and
that validation spans every packet, not one coincidence. Then, in order: the
single-threaded oracle recovers \texttt{ykusrq} at index $1{,}618{,}816{,}210$ of
$36^6$; the same key reproduces the digest of all 21 authenticated packets, each
with a distinct sequence number and payload; \autoref{tab:reveng} summarizes the
corrected parameters against the brief. Close on: 21/21 validation rules out a
single-packet coincidence.}

\paragraph{Security implications.} \ph{Main idea: what the base-36 short-key
finding means for real deployments. Then, in order: keyed-MD5 versus HMAC changes
how a candidate is computed, not how many exist, so the weakness is the size of
the space, not the MAC choice; the space is $36^6 = 2{,}176{,}782{,}336$
candidates, fully enumerable; at the rates in \autoref{sec:bench} that space falls
in minutes, so short keys over a small alphabet are the exploitable weakness.
Close on: an attacker who captures one authenticated packet can recover a short
key offline, so the digest offers little protection at this key length and
alphabet.}

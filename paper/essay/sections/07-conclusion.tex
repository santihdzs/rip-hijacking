\section{Conclusion and Deferred Work}\label{sec:conclusion}
\ph{Main idea: summary of what was recovered and how. Then, in order: we recovered
a RIPv2/RFC~2082 authentication key by reverse-engineering the keyed-MD5
construction, correcting the brief on both the MAC and the alphabet; we validated
the key against every authenticated packet in the capture; a pull-based
master--worker cluster over TCP with heartbeat- and disconnect-based failure
recovery reproduces the same key and is strongly CPU-bound. Close on: the exploit
is offline key recovery, and the result reproduces across the whole capture.}

\paragraph{Deferred: live route injection.} \ph{Main idea: the offensive
injection step is deferred by design. Then, in order: the final step is forging
an authenticated RIPv2 update with the recovered key and injecting it into a live
FRR/Mininet topology with Scapy; it is implemented as a documented stub
(\texttt{src/inject\_route.py}); it was intentionally not executed here because it
requires the Mininet sandbox. Close on: the stub in \texttt{src/inject\_route.py}
documents the injection path.}

\ph{Main idea: what a real deployment should do, grounded in the recovery cost.
Then, in order: use longer keys over a larger alphabet, since recovery cost scales
as the alphabet size raised to the key length; use per-neighbor keys with rotation
to bound blast radius and key lifetime; migrate off RIP to a protocol with
stronger authentication as the structural fix. Close on: these follow from the
recovery cost we measured and are recommendations, not defenses we evaluated.}
